1.   Most textbooks and courses in comparative politics have traditionally distinguished between industrial (and       (1)
     primarily Western) democracies — the First World; communist states as the Second World; and poor countries in
     Asia, Africa, and Latin America that had recently gained independence as the Third World. This categorization
     was based, in part, on distinctive economic features that helped characterize different political systems.
     Economic organization and performance both affected and reflected the realities of politics in these different
     political systems: the capitalist (or market) economies and high standard of living in North America, Western
     Europe, Japan, and a few other countries; the socialist (or planned) economies of countries ruled by a
     Communist Party, such as the Soviet Union and China; and the low level of economic development and
     widespread poverty characteristic of the Third World. Democracy was another dividing line in this way of looking
     at the nations of the world. The capitalist industrialized countries were not only wealthy but also stable and
     democratic; communist systems were unquestionably dictatorial; and Third World nations had a despotic leader,
     one- party rule, a military regime, or — at best — democracy marred by high levels of social and political
     violence. One of the consequences of the many profound changes of recent decades is that these economic and
     political differences among types of countries are no longer so clear. For one thing, sharp differences in growth
     rates among developing countries in recent years make it difficult to generalize with confidence about the Third
     World. Furthermore, scores of developing countries have become democratic in the past two decades. From
     Argentina to Zimbabwe, countries that were formerly undemocratic have adopted democratic institutions. Linked
     to the swelling of the ranks of democratic countries in the Third World has been the near- disappearance of
     countries in the Second World. Beginning in 1989, the collapse of communism in the former Soviet Union and
     East Central Europe set off a revolutionary change in world politics. Only a handful of countries — China, Cuba,
     Vietnam, Laos, and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North Korea) — are now ruled by communist
     parties and declare an allegiance to communist ideology. It follows that "Second World" is no longer a useful
     category by which to classify countries. These recent economic and political trends challenge and complicate
     the way that comparativists categorize the world's countries. But we believe that "Third World" remains a valid
     and useful concept and that the countries included in this text continue to share many distinct characteristics
     that allow us to identify them as part of the Third World.

    we do get dramatic pictures. The kill is the exclamation point in the day- to- day existence of the lion, since
    these great beasts spend most of their time, about 20 hours a day, sleeping and resting. Lions are social cats,
    and during these times of leisure they love to rub against each other. After drinking at a water hole, a lioness
    rests her chin on another's back. When walking past lazing members of their pride, young lions often touch
    faces with the adults, an act of bonding among members of the group. The gambling cubs make contact with all.
